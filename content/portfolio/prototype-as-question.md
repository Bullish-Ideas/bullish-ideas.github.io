This working idea starts with a simple premise: a prototype is most useful when it answers one clear question before anyone invests in a finished solution.

## Start with the uncertainty

Write the question in a way that a person can answer through use. Can a reader find the next step? Can someone tell when information is out of date? Does a sequence make sense without explanation? A focused question gives the prototype a job.

## Choose the smallest test

A sketch can test hierarchy. A clickable page can test a sequence. A short script can test how data moves through a process. Build only enough for someone to react to the question.

Before the test, decide what evidence could change the next step. Record where people pause, what they misunderstand, and which workarounds they try. If the result cannot change a decision, the prototype may not test the question you chose.

## Keep the limits visible

A prototype does not prove demand, usability, or technical quality beyond the conditions you tested. State what it covers and what it leaves unanswered. This helps readers use the result without treating an early signal as a final answer.

Treat the prototype as a question in usable form. Make the question clear, keep the test small, and let the result shape what comes next.
